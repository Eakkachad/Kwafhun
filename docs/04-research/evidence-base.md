## A. Role Models — Effect on Aspirations, Major/Career Choice

1. **Porter, C., & Serra, D. (2020).** "Gender Differences in the Choice of Major: The Importance of Female Role Models." *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 12(3), 226–254. DOI: **10.1257/app.20180426**.
   - Study type: **Field experiment (RCT-style, randomized classroom assignment)**.
   - Finding: Two successful, charismatic female econ-major alumnae gave short talks to intro econ classes at SMU; treated female students' likelihood of majoring in economics rose by **~8 percentage points** (from a 9% baseline). Effect concentrated among women who would otherwise have chosen lower-earning majors.
   - [VERIFIED] — DOI resolves, confirmed via AEA and CrossRef-equivalent (Lancaster ePrints, ScienceDaily coverage).

2. **Lawner, E. K., Quinn, D. M., Camacho, G., Johnson, B. T., & Pan-Weisz, B. (2019).** "Ingroup role models and underrepresented students' performance and interest in STEM: A meta-analysis of lab and field studies." *Social Psychology of Education*, 22, 1169–1195. DOI: **10.1007/s11218-019-09518-1**.
   - Study type: **Meta-analysis** (45 studies).
   - Finding: Small but significant positive effect of ingroup role models in **field studies (d ≈ 0.20)**; near-zero, non-significant effect in **lab studies (d ≈ 0.04)** — role model effectiveness depends heavily on real-world/ecological context, not just exposure. Interaction/attainability moderates effect.
   - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed (authors, journal, vol, pages, DOI match).

3. **Breda, T., Grenet, J., Monnet, M., & Van Effenterre, C. (2023).** "How Effective are Female Role Models in Steering Girls Towards STEM? Evidence from French High Schools." (Published in *Economic Journal* — check final venue). Working paper widely cited; large-scale RCT (~40,000 students) of female STEM role-model classroom visits in France.
   - Study type: **Large-scale RCT/field experiment**.
   - Finding: Role-model interventions shifted girls' STEM major intentions and enrollment, with effects larger for role models perceived as relatable/attainable rather than simply prestigious.
   - [UNVERIFIED — DOI not directly confirmed this session; widely cited via Paris School of Economics and PSE working paper series; recommend a direct DOI check before final citation in the deck]

4. **High-ability engineering role-model RCT, Peru** (cited via J-PAL / MacTecDraft working paper, Alba Vivar et al.).
   - Study type: **Field RCT**. Female senior engineering students gave ~20-minute talks at randomly selected high schools.
   - Finding: Increased high-ability female students' stated preference for engineering programs by **~14 percentage points**, concentrated among girls with top-quartile math scores; higher application/enrollment in engineering follow-through.
   - [UNVERIFIED] — sourced via J-PAL policy insight page and a working-paper PDF; author/year/journal not fully pinned down (likely Alba Vivar, forthcoming/working paper) — flag as promising but needs a final published-version check.

5. **Beaman, L., Duflo, E., Pande, R., & Topalova, P. (2012).** "Female Leadership Raises Aspirations and Educational Attainment for Girls: A Policy Experiment in India." *Science*, 335(6068), 582–586.
   - Study type: **Natural/policy RCT** (India's mandated female village-council leader (pradhan) reservation).
   - Finding: Exposure to female leaders closed the gender gap in adolescent aspirations and narrowed/eliminated the gender gap in parental aspirations for daughters; girls' own aspirations and educational attainment rose measurably in villages with a female-reserved leadership history.
   - [UNVERIFIED this session — could not re-run WebSearch due to rate limit, but this is a well-established, frequently-replicated citation from memory; DOI is 10.1126/science.1212382 per prior knowledge — VERIFY DOI before using in the deck].

6. **Verniers, C., Aelenei, C., Breda, T., Cimpian, J. R., Girerd, L., Molina, E., Sovet, L., & Cimpian, A. (2024).** "The Double-Edged Sword of Role Models: A Systematic Narrative Review of the Unintended Effects of Role Model Interventions on Support for the Status Quo." *Review of Educational Research* (SAGE). DOI: **10.3102/0091732X241261310**.
   - Study type: **Systematic narrative review** (55 articles).
   - Finding: Role models increase aspirants' perceived probability of success (expectancy) mainly when perceived as **attainable**; also documents a risk that role-model narratives can inadvertently reinforce "anyone can make it" status-quo narratives if not paired with structural support — a useful caveat for messaging design.
   - [VERIFIED] — DOI format matches SAGE journals record found in search.

**Design implication:** the platform should favor **attainable, relatable, field-based role-model exposure** (near-peers, recent grads, local professionals) over purely aspirational/celebrity role models, and should pair role-model content with concrete next-step actions (per the "double-edged sword" caution).

## B. Career Guidance / Career-Decision Intervention Efficacy

7. **Whiston, S. C., Li, Y., Goodrich Mitts, N., & Wright, L. (2017).** "Effectiveness of career choice interventions: A meta-analytic replication and extension." *Journal of Vocational Behavior*, 100, 175–184. DOI: **10.1016/j.jvb.2017.03.010**.
   - Study type: **Meta-analysis** (57 studies; replication/extension of Brown & Ryan Krane 2000 and Whiston, Sexton & Lasoff 1998).
   - Finding: Overall weighted mean effect size **g ≈ 0.35** for career choice interventions. Effects broken out across 7 outcome domains (vocational identity, career maturity, career decidedness, **career decision-making self-efficacy**, perceived environmental support, perceived career barriers, outcome expectations); **career decision-making self-efficacy showed the largest mean effect (~0.45)**. Significant moderators: number of sessions, hours in treatment, counselor support, values clarification, psychoeducation components.
   - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed (authors, journal, vol 100, pages 175–184, DOI matches).

8. **Whiston, S. C., Sexton, T. L., & Lasoff, D. L. (1998).** "Career-intervention outcome: A replication and extension of Oliver and Spokane (1988)." *Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 45(2), 150–165. DOI: **10.1037/0022-0167.45.2.150**.
   - Study type: **Meta-analysis** (earlier foundational replication, predecessor to #7).
   - Finding: Established that structured career interventions (workshops, courses, individual counseling) produce reliable, moderate positive effects on career-related outcomes; informs the moderator findings later confirmed by Whiston et al. 2017.
   - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed.

**Design implication:** guidance features should be **structured, multi-session where possible, and explicitly build career decision-making self-efficacy** (the outcome with the largest effect size) rather than being a single passive touchpoint — supports building CDSE-boosting exercises into the product, not just information delivery.

## C. Career Decision(-Making) Self-Efficacy (CDSE) Scales

9. **Taylor, K. M., & Betz, N. E. (1983).** "Applications of self-efficacy theory to the understanding and treatment of career indecision." *Journal of Vocational Behavior*, 22(1), 63–81.
   - Study type: **Scale-development / theory paper** (foundational CDSE Scale, 50-item, based on Crites's career maturity model and Bandura's self-efficacy theory).
   - Finding: Established the Career Decision Self-Efficacy (CDSE) construct and original 50-item scale across 5 competency domains (self-appraisal, occupational information, goal selection, planning, problem solving).
   - [VERIFIED] — confirmed via iResearchNet/Mind Garden secondary sources; DOI not resolved this session (pre-DOI era journal article, common to cite by volume/page only).
   - **Copyright note:** CDSE / CDSE-SF administration/licensing is managed commercially (Mind Garden); design must **license or adapt with permission**, not copy items verbatim.

10. **Betz, N. E., Klein, K. L., & Taylor, K. M. (1996).** "Evaluation of a Short Form of the Career Decision-Making Self-Efficacy Scale." *Journal of Career Assessment*, 4(1), 47–57.
    - Study type: **Psychometric validation study** (CDSE-SF, 25-item short form).
    - Finding: The 25-item short form retains strong reliability/validity and is far more practical for applied/product use than the original 50-item scale — this is the version most digital career products cite.
    - [UNVERIFIED this session] — could not re-confirm DOI (CrossRef rate-limited); SAGE journal record (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/106907279600400103) found in search but DOI string not independently re-verified — check before final use, likely **10.1177/106907279600400103**.
    - **Copyright note:** same as above — Mind Garden-licensed instrument; do not reproduce items without license.

11. **Luzzo, D. A. (1996).** "A Psychometric Evaluation of the Career Decision-Making Self-Efficacy Scale." *Journal of Counseling & Development*, 74(3), 276–279.
    - Study type: **Psychometric validation / construct evidence**.
    - Finding: Independent psychometric confirmation of CDSE Scale's validity, supporting its use as an outcome measure for career interventions.
    - [UNVERIFIED] — found via search snippet only, not independently DOI-checked.

**Design implication:** if the platform wants to measure impact, adapt (with license) the **CDSE-SF (25-item)** rather than building a new unvalidated scale, and cite Whiston et al. 2017's finding that CDSE is the outcome most responsive to structured intervention.

## D. Career Aspiration Formation & Broadening ("you can't be what you can't see")

12. **Gottfredson, L. S. (1981).** "Circumscription and Compromise: A Developmental Theory of Occupational Aspirations." *Journal of Counseling Psychology* (Monograph), 28(6), 545–579.
    - Study type: **Theory paper** (foundational developmental theory).
    - Finding: Children build a "cognitive map" of occupations along sex-type, prestige, and field-of-work dimensions from early childhood; aspirations are progressively **circumscribed** (narrowed) by perceived social/self boundaries well before adolescence, and later **compromised** under real-world constraints — compromising first on field of work, then prestige, least on sex-type.
    - [VERIFIED] — confirmed via ERIC record (EJ254738) and University of Delaware reprint archive; exact citation matches.
    - Companion/updated version: **Gottfredson, L. S. (2002).** "Gottfredson's theory of circumscription, compromise, and self-creation." In D. Brown (Ed.), *Career choice and development* (4th ed., pp. 85–148). San Francisco: Jossey-Bass. [VERIFIED existence via UDel reprint; standard citation for the updated theory].

13. **Markus, H., & Nurius, P. (1986).** "Possible selves." *American Psychologist*, 41(9), 954–969. DOI: **10.1037/0003-066x.41.9.954**.
    - Study type: **Theory paper** (foundational "possible selves" construct).
    - Finding: Self-concept includes not just current identity but **possible selves** — hoped-for, expected, and feared future selves — which are shaped directly by the range of roles/models a person has been exposed to; a narrow model set constrains the possible-self repertoire and thus aspiration range.
    - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed exactly (authors, journal, vol 41, pages 954–969, DOI matches).

14. **Education and Employers (2018).** "Drawing the Future: Exploring the career aspirations of primary school children from around the world." (Report, with OECD, UCL Institute of Education, NAHT, Tes.)
    - Study type: **Large-scale international survey/report** (20,000+ children aged 7–11, 20 countries).
    - Finding: Children's career aspirations are heavily gender-stereotyped and shaped overwhelmingly by **who they personally know** (parents, family friends) and by **TV/film/media**; aspirations at age 7 and age 17 are only marginally different, i.e., **narrowing happens very early**; **fewer than 1% of children reported a workplace visitor/careers exposure at school**, indicating a large gap that direct career exposure could fill.
    - [VERIFIED] — report existence, methodology, and headline stats confirmed via educationandemployers.org and WEF coverage.

**Design implication:** directly supports both the **Gottfredson-informed** design goal of intervening early (circumscription starts in childhood, not just at career-choice age) and the platform's core "broaden exposure" mechanic — the Drawing the Future finding that <1% of kids get real workplace exposure is a strong stat for the pitch.

## E. Video / Media Exposure to Careers

15. No strong RCT specifically isolating "video exposure to careers" (vs. in-person role models) was found and verified this session. Related, indirect evidence:
    - The **Drawing the Future** report (#14) documents that **TV/film/media is already one of the two dominant influences** on children's career aspirations (alongside personal acquaintance) — supports the premise that media/video exposure measurably shapes aspiration, though this is correlational/survey evidence, not an RCT of an intervention.
    - [PARTIALLY VERIFIED — the correlational claim is verified; a causal RCT specifically on video-based career exposure was not located/confirmed this session]. **Flag as a gap**: recommend a follow-up search for a dedicated video-intervention RCT (e.g., studies on "virtual work-based learning," "video-based career exposure interventions," or YouTube-based STEM career exposure studies) before citing this pillar as strongly as the others.

## F. Gamification & Self-Determination Theory (SDT) in Education

16. **Sailer, M., & Homner, L. (2020).** "The Gamification of Learning: A Meta-Analysis." *Educational Psychology Review*, 32, 77–112. (Published online 2019.) DOI: **10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w**.
    - Study type: **Meta-analysis**.
    - Finding: Significant small-to-moderate effects of gamification: **cognitive outcomes g = 0.49** (95% CI [0.30, 0.69], k=19, N=1686), **motivational outcomes g = 0.36** (95% CI [0.18, 0.54], k=16, N=2246), **behavioral outcomes g = 0.25** (95% CI [0.04, 0.46], k=9, N=951). Game fiction elements and combining competition+collaboration were especially effective for behavioral outcomes.
    - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed exactly matching (vol 32, pp 77–112, DOI).

17. **Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (1999).** "A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation." *Psychological Bulletin*, 125(6), 627–668. DOI: **10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627**.
    - Study type: **Meta-analysis** (128 studies).
    - Finding: Tangible, expected rewards significantly **undermine** free-choice intrinsic motivation — engagement-contingent d = −0.40, completion-contingent d = −0.36, performance-contingent d = −0.28. Conversely, **positive feedback/praise increases** free-choice motivation (**d ≈ +0.33**) and self-reported interest (d ≈ +0.31). Strongly supports Cognitive Evaluation Theory / SDT: controlling tangible rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation, informational/positive feedback enhances it.
    - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed exactly (vol 125, pp 627–668, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627). Note: our brief's "d ≈ −0.28 to −0.40" range is precisely correct.

18. **Hanus, M. D., & Fox, J. (2015).** "Assessing the effects of gamification in the classroom: A longitudinal study on intrinsic motivation, social comparison, satisfaction, effort, and academic performance." *Computers & Education*, 80, 152–161. DOI: **10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019**. (Corrigendum: DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2018.09.019, Dec 2018 — check corrigendum content before citing exact numbers.)
    - Study type: **Longitudinal field quasi-experiment** (two real course sections, 16 weeks).
    - Finding: The gamified course (badges + leaderboard) showed **decreased** intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, and empowerment relative to the non-gamified control course over the semester — a cautionary finding on poorly-designed competitive/tangible-reward gamification, consistent with Deci et al.'s SDT-based prediction.
    - [VERIFIED] — CrossRef record confirmed; note a corrigendum exists and should be checked for any correction to reported statistics before using specific numbers in the deck.

19. Recent post-2019 gamification meta-analysis (K-12 focus): search surfaced **"Effects of Gamification on Behavioral Change in Education: A Meta-Analysis"** (PMC/NCBI, PMC8037535) as a promising 2021-era follow-up meta-analysis specifically on behavioral outcomes.
    - [UNVERIFIED — full citation (authors/journal/DOI) not confirmed this session; recommend fetching PMC8037535 directly to extract full citation before use].

**Design implication:** this is the strongest-evidenced pillar. The deck's SDT-based design choice ("real opportunities as rewards, not badges/points") is well-supported: Deci et al. shows tangible/controlling rewards suppress intrinsic motivation while positive feedback enhances it, and Hanus & Fox is a concrete field demonstration of the badges/leaderboard failure mode Sailer & Homner's positive meta-analytic average can mask.

## G. Explainable / Deterministic Recommendation vs. Black-Box ML (Education, Child-Facing, High-Stakes Contexts)

20. This pillar is **thin**, as anticipated in the brief. No education-specific RCT or meta-analysis was located and verified this session directly comparing deterministic/rule-based vs. black-box ML recommenders for child-facing career guidance. What exists (not independently verified this session, offered as leads only):
    - General AI-in-education literature and UNESCO/OECD policy guidance broadly argue for **transparency and explainability** in AI systems used with minors, on grounds of accountability, contestability, and avoiding embedded bias — but this is **policy/ethics guidance, not empirical evidence of better outcomes**, and specific citations were not verified this session.
    - The broader XAI (explainable AI) literature (e.g., Doshi-Velez & Kim 2017 "Towards a Rigorous Science of Interpretable Machine Learning") is a standard general argument for interpretability in high-stakes decisions, but is domain-general, not education/child-specific.
    - **Recommendation**: state in the pitch deck that the deterministic/constraint-engine choice is justified primarily by (a) auditability/explainability requirements for child-facing high-stakes guidance (an ethics/product argument, not yet a strong empirical evidence base) and (b) avoidance of black-box bias amplification — and **flag this pillar explicitly as design-rationale rather than evidence-backed** in the deck, since no strong verified academic RCT/meta-analysis was found in this session's search budget.
    - [UNVERIFIED / GAP] — recommend a dedicated follow-up literature search (e.g., "explainable AI education recommender systems," "algorithmic transparency EdTech minors," "interpretable ML high-stakes decisions education") if this pillar needs to appear as a cited academic claim rather than a design-philosophy statement.
